Why will we succeed?

High replay value

Every show is unique. 9 of the 11 characters have two possible arcs, creating 18 potential character outcomes, all feeding into 4 possible endings. With so many branching paths, a single visit offers only a fractional glimpse of the show’s full universe. During our development run, three guests who attended opening night came back the next day to experience it again.

Provocative marketing

In our first run, we sold out our show but did very little marketing. Everything Immersive featured us on their website and word generally spread from there, through immersive theater networks and word-of-mouth. In honor of the widespread use of LSD on the set of The Holy Mountain, we invited participants to bring their own prescription ketamine to take as a sacrament during the show. This generated controversy on Reddit and spiked our ticket sales.

Immersive is a loyal niche

Immersive theater has an unusually devoted audience. NYC in particular has a dense community of immersive enthusiasts who constantly seek out new work. When they find a show they love, they come back again and again, sometimes dozens of times, as with Sleep No More and Death of Rasputin. They are especially drawn to rich worlds and experiences that reward exploration; shows with multiple storylines, layers, and symbolic depth tend to generate fandoms who attend the show’s events and chatter on their discords and subreddits.

In our first run, we found that P9 appealed to a range of personas:

  • Immersive theater loyalist
  • Film nerd
  • Game nerd (LARP, escape rooms, D&D)
  • High-openness autist
  • Burning Man/House of Yes crowd
  • New Age community
  • Tech and finance yuppie looking for a novelty experience
  • Tourist
  • Jodorowsky fan
What makes Psychomagic 9 unique?

Real stakes, real consequences, real interactivity

The show feels like being in a movie or an open-world video game. Characters give you tasks, completing them—if you choose to—influences their arc and the overall shape of the plot. Participation isn’t cheesy and artificial but rather psychoactive and consequential. Guests partake in rituals, games, social experiments, private 1x1s, small collusions, and good clean honest labor.

Boundary-pushing

Audiences are bored. They want peak experiences, delicious surprises, and fresh encounters that resonate with meaning. P9 doesn’t just script these things in; it is designed to allow them to arise organically. The show invites the participant to push past the usual boundaries in a safe container where anything feels possible. This combination of freedom and trust is unusual in immersive, and it tends to generate wild stories and unexpected emergent behavior that guests still talk about months later.

  • A man who later told us “I lost my sense of reality” stood up to a mob of feminist bullies attacking the main character, then voluntarily jailed himself for a portion of the show in solidarity.
    • And offered to take the lead actor’s scripted punishment with kamikaze-like devotion to the cause
  • A guest spontaneously faked a seizure to help a character steal a bottle of wine. Afterwards he told us, “I’ve been wanting to do that my entire life.”
  • Someone decided to call an old sex hotline from the 80s for Scumpo, the homeless sex pest living in the walls of the set. When they realized the line was still active, they used their own credit card to purchase minutes so that Scumpo could talk to the hotline worker.

More than a show

P9 is more than a show. It’s a world. You can sort of think of it as an open-ended playground that easily spawns new characters, stories, experiences, and even revenue streams beyond the core production.

Extensible cast. P9 is uniquely suited to guest improvisers and celebrity cameos. Talented improvisers can enter the fray and interact with the existing cast and storyline with little to no rehearsal. An additional performer generates enough content for ~15 extra audience members but only needs to be paid for the performances they appear in. This keeps the cost of NPCs low while increasing audience capacity and opening up opportunities for celebrity marketing.

Satellite events. The McKittrick (one of our potential venues) wants shows that can generate nightlife content. We have a few simple but high-impact event ideas taking place in the world of P9 that we expect to pilot (e.g. Tarot Blackjack Cabaret, a karmic casino where you gamble for your salvation in improvised tarot song).

Venue

We have two venues informally on standby for the show.

The first is The Culture Club, formerly The McKittrick, home of Sleep No More. We are in talks with Matthew Kwatinetz about renting one floor (10k sq ft) for approximately $50k/month. It has a great Manhattan location and we would be able to split drink revenue from their speakeasy bar.

Our second option is a former convent in Bushwick, owned by a friend. At around half the cost but a similar size, it would give us greater control over the space and more flexibility in shaping the experience.

Both are excited to have P9 if our timelines and funding line up.

Case Study

We are building on the model of Death of Rasputin, an immersive sandbox show that ran for 12 weeks on Governors Island in 2025. They were a nonprofit production with a budget of $1m and a crew of 50. With $140 tickets, the show generated approximately $1 million in ticket and bar revenue, sold out every performance, and earned strong reviews.

The production is now being remounted for-profit at the McKittrick. It gives us a model for a production very similar to P9 that started as a successful nonprofit run and developed into a commercially sustainable show.

FAQ
Why are you producing P9 as a nonprofit?

We estimate that P9 would need at least eight months to become profitable. Committing to a year-long lease before validating demand would expose us to significant financial risk, particularly given all the uncertainty in the world right now. A nonprofit run allows us to establish the show, build an audience, and demonstrate commercial viability. We are following the model of Death of Rasputin (see Budget & Funding above).

If the run is successful, will you extend it?

Maybe! That will depend on the terms of our contract with the venue. We would certainly consider it, but we would weigh an extension against the (perhaps more strategic) option of a limited run, which would allow us to test the market, build an audience, and generate anticipation for a reboot. It would also allow us to restructure the show as a for-profit.

What is the future of the show?

Our longer term goal is to remount P9 as a for-profit with an extended run of at least 12 months. We would pursue this only if the three-month run demonstrates strong audience demand and commercial potential.

What happens if you don’t raise enough money for this run?

If we raise less than $500k, funds will be redirected toward other Mischief and Glory, Inc. projects.

If we raise at least $500k, but less than our target, we will move forward with a leaner version of P9. We’ll simplify scenic and design, shorten the rehearsal/playtesting period to one month (less leisurely, but still doable), and potentially secure a cheaper venue, unless projected drink revenue at the Culture Club is high enough to offset the gap.

We would also expand our audience capacity. Our current target of 100 guests/show prioritizes intimacy and deep interactivity, allowing most audience members to engage with the show as a malleable, individually co-creative system. We think this format is especially psychoactive and we would be sad to compromise it. But we could find a middle ground by decreasing individual interactions and adding more scripted scenes and group activities, for a target capacity of 120–150. This would sacrifice some of the intimacy and strangeness that make P9 distinctive, but would preserve the core experience while significantly increasing revenue.
